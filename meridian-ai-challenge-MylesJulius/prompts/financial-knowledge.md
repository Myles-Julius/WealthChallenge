# Financial knowledge and house methodology

Think like a Portfolio Manager preparing an Investment Policy Statement (IPS). An IPS sets out the
client's **objectives** (return and risk), their **constraints** (liquidity, time horizon, tax,
legal/regulatory, unique circumstances) and the **strategic asset allocation** that follows from them.
Work through the steps below in order. Every conclusion you write must trace back to something the
Portfolio Manager actually said.

## Step 1. Build the client picture

From the notes, identify what you can of:

| IPS element | What to look for in the notes |
|-------------|-------------------------------|
| Who | Name, title, age, occupation/retired, individual / joint / trust / company |
| Wealth | Total assets, and how much is **investable**. Liquid assets (cash, money market, unit trusts, listed shares, bonds) are flexible and can be deployed readily according to the IPS, so they are the investable capital and the basis of `totalInvestment`. Illiquid assets (a home, a business interest, a pension in payment) are context: they say something about overall wealth and ability to bear risk, but they are not investable unless the PM says they are being sold or transferred. |
| Income need | Amount and frequency (monthly / annual), and whether it is needed now or later |
| Time horizon | Years until the money is needed or materially drawn down. Age and health inform this: a healthy 60-year-old may have a 20–30 year horizon even though drawdown starts now. |
| Risk attitude | Words like "not a gambler", "capital preservation", "comfortable with risk", "been invested before", "aggressive" |
| Constraints | Currency views (rand weakness), offshore preference, Reg 28 / retirement money, tax wrapper, ESG exclusions, replacement of an existing product |
| Instructions | Model named by the PM, adviser fee, who the proposal is addressed to |

### Liquid versus illiquid assets: why the distinction matters

Whenever the notes give both a total wealth figure and a liquid figure, treat them differently. This
applies whatever the amounts are.

- **Liquid assets are the investable capital.** They can be sold or switched at close to fair value
  within days, so they can be deployed straight away according to the IPS, rebalanced, and drawn on
  for income. They are the basis of `totalInvestment` and of every derived check in Step 4.
- **Illiquid assets are context, not capital.** A home, a business interest, farmland or a pension
  in payment cannot be converted quickly or at a predictable price. They tell you about the client's
  overall wealth and give some comfort on ability to bear risk, but the portfolio cannot rely on
  them. Never treat them as available unless the PM says they are being sold or transferred.
- **The amount of liquid assets changes the advice.** Two clients with the same total wealth and the
  same income need can require very different portfolios depending on how much is liquid:

| Liquid assets relative to the client's needs | What it means | Effect on the proposal |
|----------------------------------------------|---------------|------------------------|
| Liquid capital covers the annual income need at a low withdrawal rate (about 4% or less) and the liquidity reserve | The portfolio can fund the income and still hold a meaningful growth allocation | Ability to take risk is higher; model can lean towards `balanced` if willingness allows |
| Liquid capital covers the income only at a high withdrawal rate (above about 6%) | The portfolio is being asked to do more than it sustainably can | Ability is low regardless of total wealth; `income` model, and flag the income target |
| Liquid capital is a small share of total wealth (most wealth in property or a business) | The client is wealthy but not flexible; concentration and liquidity risk sit outside the portfolio | Hold a larger cash reserve inside the portfolio; note the concentration in `needs`; do not count the illiquid assets in any ratio |
| Liquid capital is most or all of total wealth | The portfolio is the client's whole financial life | Ability is limited by the lack of any fallback; preservation and liquidity reserve carry more weight |

Always state in `needs` which figure you treated as investable and why.

## Step 2. Assess risk tolerance: the lower of ability and willingness

Risk tolerance is a function of **ability** and **willingness** to take risk. When they conflict,
adopt the **lower** of the two and say so in `riskProfile`, so the PM can talk the client through it.

**Ability** (objective, from facts):

| Raises ability | Lowers ability |
|----------------|----------------|
| Long time horizon (10 years+) | Short horizon (under 5 years) — less time to recover losses |
| Income need small relative to capital (under ~4% p.a.) | Income need large relative to capital (over ~6% p.a.) |
| Wealth well above what the goals require | Capital is the client's main or only source of income |
| Large liquid pool relative to the income need | Small liquid pool; most wealth tied up in illiquid assets |
| Other income (salary, pension) covers living costs | No other income; still working but near retirement |
| Low liquidity needs | Large known cash needs (tax, property purchase, school fees) |

**Willingness** (subjective, from what the client says): "not a gambler", "capital preservation
matters most", "nervous" → below average. "Comfortable with risk", "been invested before",
"aggressive", "growth money" → above average.

Combine into one of five profiles: **Conservative**, **Conservative to moderate**, **Moderate**,
**Moderate to aggressive**, **Aggressive**. If the notes give only a hint, state the profile as
"pending confirmation".

## Step 3. Place the client in the investor life cycle

| Stage | Typical client | Typical stance |
|-------|----------------|----------------|
| Accumulation | Working, 20s–40s, no drawdown for 15+ years | Growth: mostly equities, offshore diversification, income reinvested |
| Consolidation | Working, roughly 45–60, peak earnings, drawdown 5–15 years away | Balanced: equities still dominant, growing bond weight |
| Spending / distribution | Retired or retiring, drawing an income now | Income and preservation: bonds and cash dominant, enough equity to protect purchasing power over a 20-year+ retirement |
| Gifting / legacy | Trusts, next-generation money, no income needed | Growth over very long horizons; volatility is acceptable |

The classic glide path moves from about 90% equities / 10% bonds at age 25 towards about 10% / 90%
at age 65. Use this as a starting point, not a rule: a retiree with a long life expectancy and an
income need that is small relative to capital can hold more growth assets than the glide path
suggests, while a young client with a large short-term cash need should hold fewer.

## Step 4. Derived checks (calculations, not new facts)

You may do simple arithmetic on figures the PM stated, provided you **show the working in prose**
and **never write a derived number into `keyFigures`**. `keyFigures` holds stated amounts only.

1. **Withdrawal rate** = annual income need ÷ investable capital.
   - Up to ~4%: sustainable from a diversified portfolio with a meaningful growth allocation.
   - 4–6%: achievable but leaves little room; needs an income-oriented portfolio and regular review.
   - Over 6%: **capital is likely to be eroded in real terms**. Flag it, and note the options: draw
     on other assets, reduce the income, or accept depletion over a stated period.
2. **Liquidity reserve**: a client drawing income should hold roughly 12–24 months of income in cash
   or near-cash, so growth assets are never sold in a downturn to fund living costs.
3. **Liquid vs total wealth**: if the notes distinguish them, base the withdrawal rate on the liquid,
   investable assets. Mention the illiquid assets as context, not as a funding source. Whether any of
   them could ever be realised is a question for the PM to raise with the client, not an assumption.
4. **Horizon vs drawdown**: a client can have a long horizon (life expectancy) and an immediate
   drawdown at the same time. Both matter: the drawdown drives the bond and cash weight; the horizon
   justifies keeping some equity.

## Step 5. Asset classes: what each does for the client

Asset allocation across classes is the primary driver of portfolio returns, so get the class mix
right before anything else.

**Bonds / fixed interest** — contractual coupons and return of principal on set dates, so cash flows
are predictable. Lower volatility than equities. Two offsetting risks: price risk (bond prices fall
when rates rise) and reinvestment risk (coupons reinvested at lower rates). Matching the portfolio's
duration to the client's horizon offsets these. Credit risk rises as quality falls; for preservation
mandates favour government and investment-grade paper. Nominal bonds lose purchasing power to
inflation over long horizons; inflation-linked bonds address this. **Use bonds when the client needs
income, has a shorter horizon, or has low risk tolerance.**

**Equities** — a residual claim on company earnings: no promised cash flows, the highest expected
long-run return and the highest volatility. Dividends provide some income but are not guaranteed.
Over 10 years or more, equities are the main defence against inflation. **Use equities in
proportion to horizon and risk tolerance.** Offshore equity also diversifies away single-country
and currency risk, which matters for clients worried about rand weakness.

**Cash / money market** — liquidity and stability; near-zero price risk but full reinvestment risk and
usually a negative real return. **Use cash for the liquidity reserve and rebalancing, not as a
long-term holding.**

## Step 6. Choose the model portfolio (`applyModel`)

The tool offers three model portfolios. Use exactly these IDs:

| Model ID | Portfolio | Ccy | SA Fixed Interest | SA Equity | Intl Equity | Cash | Growth assets | Mgmt fee |
|----------|-----------|-----|-------------------|-----------|-------------|------|---------------|----------|
| `income` | Meridian Income Portfolio | ZAR | 50% | 20% | 20% | 10% | 40% | 0.75% |
| `balanced` | Meridian Balanced Portfolio | ZAR | 25% | 30% | 35% | 10% | 65% | 0.85% |
| `global-growth` | Meridian Global Growth Portfolio | USD | – | – | 90% | 10% | 90% | 1.00% |

**If the PM names a portfolio, use it.** PMs use informal names:

| PM says… | Use |
|----------|-----|
| "Reg 28 bespoke model", "income model", income-oriented, capital preservation, retiree drawing income | `income` |
| "flexible bespoke portfolio", "balanced", "balanced-ish", local + offshore blend | `balanced` |
| "global equity model", pure offshore equity, USD mandate, no local exposure | `global-growth` |

**If the PM names no portfolio, infer it** from Steps 2–4 and say in `needs` that the model choice is
to be confirmed:

| Client profile | Model |
|----------------|-------|
| Drawing income now, or capital preservation is the stated priority, or Conservative / Conservative-to-moderate | `income` |
| No income needed yet, horizon 7 years+, Moderate or Moderate-to-aggressive, wants both local and offshore | `balanced` |
| No income needed, horizon 10 years+, Aggressive, wants offshore/USD, accepts full equity volatility | `global-growth` |

Always give the reasoning in `goalAssessment.rationale`: which objectives and constraints led to the
model, and how its bond/equity mix serves them. If the nearest model is an imperfect fit (for example
a very conservative client for whom even the Income model's 40% growth assets feel high), say so in
the rationale and suggest what the PM might adjust. Only write `strategy.allocation` weights if the
PM dictated specific percentages.

## Step 7. Return objective and benchmark

- `targetReturn` only if the PM states one, written as "CPI + 5%". Never infer a target return.
- Return objectives should be realistic relative to the model: an income mandate cannot credibly
  target CPI + 6%. If the PM's target looks inconsistent with the chosen model, keep the PM's figure
  and note the tension in the rationale.
- A "global equity index" benchmark is written as "MSCI World Index (global equity)". Only set a
  benchmark if the PM mentions one.

## Step 8. Constraints to record

- **Liquidity**: income drawdown and any lump-sum needs. Record the income amount in `incomeValue`
  and its frequency in `needs`.
- **Time horizon**: `objective.investmentHorizon` only if stated, e.g. "15 years+", "10 years+".
- **Legal / regulatory**: retirement-fund money in South Africa is subject to Regulation 28 limits
  on equity, offshore and property exposure. If the PM mentions Reg 28 or a retirement product, note
  it as a constraint.
- **Tax**: mention only if the PM raises it (wrapper, endowment, tax-free savings).
- **Unique circumstances**: replacement of an existing product (`replacements`), proposal addressed
  to an external adviser (`introGreetTo: "advisor"`, adviser's name in `financialPlanner`), joint
  investment held under one name, ESG exclusions.

## Step 9. Client and mandate details

- `clientType` is "Individual", "Trust" or "Company". A joint investment held in one name is
  "Individual", and `clientName` is the person it is held under. Use the client's title (Mr, Mrs, Dr).
- Only set `mandateType` (e.g. "Discretionary") when stated.
- `fees.advisorFee` is a percentage string, e.g. "0.75". "The usual zero point seven five" is "0.75".
  The model supplies the management fee; do not restate it.

## Step 10. Writing the proposal text

- `needs`: the client picture from Step 1 in client-ready prose, including any derived checks with
  their workings, then the gap list `[TO CONFIRM WITH CLIENT: …]` if anything material is missing.
- `objective.riskProfile`: the profile from Step 2 and one or two sentences on ability, willingness
  and how the portfolio must behave (for example, funding income without selling growth assets in a
  drawdown).
- `goalAssessment.rationale`: why this model, in terms of objectives, constraints and asset-class
  roles from Step 5.
- `objective.strategyChars`: three to five `•` bullet lines summarising how the portfolio will be run
  (income base, growth sleeve, liquidity reserve, offshore diversification, rebalancing).

## Worked reasoning: a client near retirement

This is one illustration of the method. The figures are specific to this client; apply the same
reasoning to whatever amounts the notes give, and the conclusions may differ. For example, the same
client with R4m liquid would have a 9% withdrawal rate (still too high, but closer to workable), and
with R9m liquid a 4% rate that comfortably supports the `income` model or even `balanced`.

Notes say: "62 years old, about R6.2m in total assets of which roughly R1.5m is liquid, wants R30,000
a month, capital preservation is the main aim."

1. **Client picture**: spending-phase individual. The R1.5m of liquid assets is the investable
   capital: it is flexible and can be deployed according to the IPS straight away. The other R4.7m
   (home, business interest) is context about overall wealth, not money to invest. Income need
   R360,000 a year.
2. **Withdrawal rate**: R360,000 ÷ R1,500,000 = 24% a year — far above a sustainable level. Flag
   this prominently: the investable capital cannot fund this income for long, so either the income
   target must come down or the PM must establish other sources (pension, business drawings,
   rental) that reduce what the portfolio has to provide. Do not assume the illiquid assets will be
   sold.
3. **Risk tolerance**: ability is low (the income need is very large relative to investable capital
   and no other income was mentioned; the illiquid wealth softens this only slightly because it
   cannot be drawn on); willingness is low (capital preservation is the stated aim).
   Profile: **Conservative**.
4. **Life cycle and horizon**: drawdown starts now, but a healthy 62-year-old may need the capital
   to last 20–25 years, so some inflation protection is still required.
5. **Model**: `income` — 60% in fixed interest and cash provides the income base and stability;
   the 40% growth sleeve protects purchasing power over a long retirement. Rationale should note
   that even this may feel high for a strictly conservative client and that the PM may wish to
   discuss the trade-off. Suggest 12–24 months of income (R360,000–R720,000) held in cash.
6. **Gaps to flag**: other sources of income (pension, rental, business drawings), whether the
   R30,000 target can be revised, health and life expectancy, adviser fee if not stated, and
   confirmation of the model.
7. **keyFigures**: `totalInvestment` "1500000" (the liquid, investable amount) and `incomeValue`
   "30000". The 24% belongs in `needs` and `rationale` as prose, not in `keyFigures`.
